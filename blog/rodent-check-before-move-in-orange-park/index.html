A property that has sat vacant for a while, between tenants, during a sale, or while a seasonal owner was away, is one of the more common sources of a rodent surprise for someone moving in. Nobody was checking the garage, the attic or the yard regularly, which gives mice and rats exactly the kind of undisturbed time they need to settle in unnoticed.

Why vacancy specifically matters.

An occupied home gets disturbed constantly, doors opening, footsteps, lights, cooking smells, all of which make a space less appealing for a rodent looking to settle somewhere quiet. A vacant property offers none of that disruption, which is part of why a home or rental unit that sat empty for weeks or months is worth checking more carefully than one that was continuously occupied until the day you moved in.

This applies across Orange Park regardless of whether the property is a long-term rental, a home between sales, or a seasonal property near Doctors Lake that sits empty for stretches of the year.

What to check before or right after moving in.

  • Garage corners and stored-item areas for droppings or gnaw marks.
  • Under sinks and around plumbing penetrations in the kitchen and bathrooms.
  • The attic hatch area and any visible insulation for disturbance.
  • Exterior garage door seals and foundation vents for obvious gaps.
Nobody was checking a vacant property for the last few months. That is exactly the point.

What a pre-move-in inspection actually covers.

Rodent inspection and estimates before or immediately after moving in covers the same full walk-through used for any other call, exterior perimeter, attic, garage and interior areas where signs would show up first. For a rental specifically, having a documented inspection on record is also useful if a dispute later comes up about pre-existing versus new damage.

If activity or damage is found, it gets documented clearly, which matters for a renter dealing with a landlord or a buyer who just closed on a home, since clear documentation of what was found and when helps establish responsibility for the issue going forward.

What this looks like for a landlord or property manager.

For a landlord managing a vacant unit between tenants, a check before the new tenant moves in is a reasonable standard practice, both to catch any issue before it becomes the new tenant's problem and to document the unit's condition clearly for the transition. This is especially relevant for properties that sat vacant for an extended marketing or renovation period.

A landlord with multiple Orange Park rental properties sometimes benefits from a standing policy, an inspection scheduled automatically whenever a unit sits vacant past a certain number of weeks, rather than relying on a tenant to report a problem after the fact. That kind of proactive scheduling tends to catch activity while it is still a single entry point and a light population, rather than after it has had months to grow undisturbed.

Seasonal and vacation properties near the water.

Orange Park has a share of seasonal or vacation properties near Doctors Lake that sit unoccupied for stretches of the year, sometimes months at a time between visits. These properties combine two risk factors already discussed elsewhere, extended vacancy and proximity to water, which makes a pre-arrival check worth treating as a routine part of opening the property up again rather than an occasional afterthought.

If you own or manage a seasonal property near the water, scheduling a check a few days before a planned stay is a smart move. It tends to make for a considerably less stressful start to the visit than discovering a problem after arriving with belongings and family in tow.

What to do if something is found.

If a pre-move-in check finds activity.

  • Get the findings documented clearly with photos before moving belongings in.
  • Address active animals and entry points before storing items in an affected garage or attic.
  • Confirm with a landlord, if applicable, who is responsible for the cost.
  • Schedule a follow-up visit to confirm the issue has actually resolved.

Finding something before you have unpacked everything into a garage or attic is considerably easier to deal with than discovering it weeks later once belongings are already stored in an affected space. A pre-move-in check is a small step that avoids a much larger cleanup later.

What to do, and not do, while you wait

General safety practice drawn from CDC guidance on rodents. It is meant to keep you safe before any professional arrives.

  • Never dry sweep or vacuum droppings, urine stains or nesting material.
  • Air out a closed attic, garage or crawl space for at least 30 minutes before you go in to work.
  • Put on gloves before touching anything contaminated, then wash your hands thoroughly when you finish.
  • Soak the droppings and the surface in disinfectant first. Wiping comes only after the soak time.
  • Double check that waste is bagged and sealed, and never pick up a dead rodent with bare hands.
  • Flu-like symptoms or shortness of breath after rodent exposure are a reason to see a doctor and mention the cleanup. Health questions belong with a physician, not a pest company.

Sources and standards

Public guidance behind the advice on this page. It describes general practice and rules, not a claim about any one provider.

  • CDC: How to Clean Up After Rodents: The public-health steps for airing out a space, wetting droppings with disinfectant instead of sweeping, and bagging waste.

Common questions

Is a pre-move-in check worth it even if I have not seen any signs?

Yes, especially for a property that sat vacant for a while, since the absence of obvious signs does not rule out activity that has not yet been noticed.

Who is responsible for a rodent problem found right after moving in?

That depends on the lease or sale terms, but documentation from an inspection helps establish when the issue was actually found, which is useful for resolving that question.

How long should a property sit vacant before a check becomes worth it?

Even a few weeks of vacancy is enough to be worth a check, and longer vacancies, a seasonal property or an extended sale process, make it more worthwhile still.

Should a seasonal property near the water be checked every time before a visit?

It is a reasonable standing habit, since the combination of extended vacancy and waterfront proximity makes these properties more likely to see activity develop unnoticed between visits.

Does a brand new construction home still need a pre-move-in check?

It is lower risk than an older vacant property, but a short period of vacancy between final construction and move-in is still enough time for an entry point to go unnoticed, so a quick check remains worthwhile.
