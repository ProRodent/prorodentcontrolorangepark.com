Garage Rodent Signs in Orange Park's Slab-Built Homes.

Without a true basement, the garage carries most of the lower-level rodent pressure a typical Orange Park home sees.

Most homes in Orange Park, like much of North Florida, are built on a concrete slab rather than over a basement. That construction style changes where rodent pressure actually shows up compared to a house with a true below-grade level, and the garage ends up carrying a lot of the lower-level activity that a basement would handle in other parts of the country.

Why garages see so much activity here.

A garage gets opened and closed regularly for the car, but the stored items along the walls, holiday decorations, yard equipment, boxes that have not been touched in years, often go unchecked for long stretches. That undisturbed storage is exactly the kind of space mice and rats look for, and a garage door seal that has worn down over years of daily use is one of the most common entry points we find on Orange Park calls.

Utility penetrations where water, electrical or gas lines enter the garage from outside are another common gap, especially on older homes where the original sealing material has degraded over the decades in Florida's humidity.

What to actually look for.

  • Droppings along the base of stored boxes or shelving.
  • Gnaw marks on cardboard, plastic bins or stored equipment.
  • A visible gap or daylight showing under the garage door when closed.
  • Chewed insulation or wiring near a water heater or HVAC equipment.
A garage door seal worn down over years is an easy way in that nobody notices until something is already inside.

Why this matters more without a basement.

In a house with a basement, below-grade activity often gets caught earlier simply because people go down there regularly for laundry or storage. A slab-built Orange Park home does not have that same lower level, so the garage becomes the default space where early signs would otherwise be noticed in a basement-equipped house elsewhere.

This is one reason garage and basement rodent control calls are common here even though almost none of our Orange Park properties have an actual basement. The garage, utility room and crawl space under the house together take on that role.

What a thorough garage check actually involves.

A technician checks the garage door seal and threshold specifically, not just a glance at the floor for droppings. Utility penetrations get traced from where the line enters the garage to where it continues into the house, since a gap at that transition point is easy to miss on a casual look.

Stored items get moved or checked where droppings or gnaw marks suggest activity, rather than leaving boxes stacked against a wall unexamined. If wiring near a water heater or electrical panel shows chew damage, that gets documented honestly as its own item under rodent damage repair.

Crawl spaces under the house play a similar role.

Beyond the garage itself, many Orange Park homes have a crawl space underneath rather than a slab poured directly on grade, especially on older construction. That crawl space sees even less foot traffic than a garage, since almost nobody enters it regularly, which makes it another undisturbed area worth checking alongside the garage rather than assuming the garage alone tells the whole story.

A crawl space access panel that has not been opened in years is worth a look during a thorough inspection, particularly if garage activity has already been confirmed. The same population may be using both spaces interchangeably depending on which offers easier access on a given night.

Simple habits that help.

Reducing garage rodent risk going forward.

  • Store items in sealed plastic bins rather than cardboard boxes.
  • Check the garage door seal periodically for wear or gaps.
  • Keep stored food, including pet food, in sealed containers.
  • Walk the garage perimeter occasionally rather than only using the car bay.

None of these habits replace a proper inspection if activity is already present, but they reduce how inviting the space is in the first place, which matters in a slab-built home where the garage carries more of the lower-level pressure than it would elsewhere.

Attached versus detached garages.

An attached garage shares a wall and often a few utility penetrations directly with the living space, which means activity there has a shorter path to the rest of the house than a detached garage set apart from the main structure. A detached garage still deserves the same inspection attention, but activity confined to it is less likely to spread indoors on its own without a separate entry point into the house itself.

Before you call: safe steps for rodent signs

This is general public-health guidance from the CDC, not a description of any one job. It applies whether you call us or anyone else.

  • Never dry sweep or vacuum droppings, urine stains or nesting material.
  • Air out a closed attic, garage or crawl space for at least 30 minutes before you go in to work.
  • Gloves on first, handwashing last. Do both every time.
  • Soak the droppings and the surface in disinfectant first. Wiping comes only after the soak time.
  • Bag the waste, seal the bag, and put it in the trash. Do not handle live or dead rodents with bare hands.
  • Flu-like symptoms or shortness of breath after rodent exposure are a reason to see a doctor and mention the cleanup. Health questions belong with a physician, not a pest company.

Common questions

Why does my garage get more activity than the rest of the house?

Without a true basement, the garage becomes the default lower-level space, and worn door seals or utility gaps give rodents an easy route in that often goes unnoticed for a while.

Can rodents damage my water heater or electrical panel?

They can chew wiring and insulation near either, which is why those specific areas get checked directly during a garage inspection.

Do sealed bins really make a difference?

Yes, sealed plastic bins are far less inviting for nesting than cardboard, though closing the actual entry point is still the more important fix.

Should I check the crawl space under the house too?

Yes, especially if garage activity has already been confirmed. A crawl space that has not been opened in a while can hold the same population using the garage, and both are worth checking together.
