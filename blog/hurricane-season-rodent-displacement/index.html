Florida's storm season brings a specific rodent pattern that homeowners further from the coast or from low-lying areas do not usually have to think about: displacement. When heavy rain floods burrows, drainage areas and low-lying yards, the rodents living there do not simply disappear, they move, and the nearest dry, sheltered structure is often the obvious next stop.

Why flooding changes rodent behavior specifically.

Norway rats in particular often live in burrow systems along fence lines, drainage ditches or low vegetation near water, which is common around Doctors Lake and the St. Johns River frontage in Orange Park. When sustained heavy rain floods those burrows, the animals inside are displaced immediately, and unlike a slow seasonal shift, this kind of displacement can happen within a single storm event rather than over weeks.

A garage, shed or crawl space that sits higher and drier than the surrounding yard becomes an obvious destination for a displaced rat looking for immediate shelter, which is why calls sometimes spike noticeably in the days immediately following a significant storm.

What to watch for after a heavy storm.

  • New burrow openings or disturbed soil along a fence line after flooding recedes.
  • Sudden indoor activity that was not present before the storm.
  • Droppings appearing in a garage or crawl space shortly after heavy rain.
  • Increased activity along any structure sitting higher than the surrounding yard.
A flooded burrow does not stop needing shelter just because the water came up fast.

Why pre-storm prevention matters.

Sealing existing gaps before storm season reduces how easily a displaced rodent can get into your home or garage during exactly the moment it is most motivated to find shelter quickly. Rodent exclusion and entry point sealing done ahead of a known risk period is simpler and cheaper than dealing with an active indoor population that moved in during a storm.

For properties near the water specifically, checking fence lines and low-lying yard areas for existing burrow activity before storm season gives a sense of how much displaced pressure a given property might see if flooding occurs. That is useful information even without any current indoor concern.

What to do during active storm conditions.

During active flooding or immediately after, it is reasonable to expect more outdoor rodent movement than usual, and that movement does not always mean your specific property has a new problem, just that displaced animals are moving through the area looking for shelter. Watching for new signs over the following days, rather than assuming every storm automatically brings a lasting infestation, is a reasonable approach.

Safety comes first during active flooding, and checking for rodent activity is never worth risking a trip through standing water or storm debris. Whatever displacement is happening in the yard will still be there to assess once conditions are actually safe to walk the property, and that short delay does not meaningfully change the outcome.

Why Orange Park's specific geography matters here.

Orange Park's position along Doctors Lake and the St. Johns River means a meaningful share of properties sit close enough to water to see this displacement pattern directly, more so than a town further inland with less water frontage overall. That does not mean every Orange Park property floods during a storm, but it does mean the displacement pattern discussed here is a real, recurring consideration for this specific area rather than a generic warning that applies everywhere equally.

After the storm passes.

A reasonable post-storm check.

  • Walk the yard perimeter once it is safe to do so, looking for new burrow activity.
  • Check the garage and any sheds for new droppings or nesting material.
  • Note whether any new sounds or signs appeared specifically after the storm.
  • Schedule an inspection if activity seems to be settling in rather than passing through.

Not every bit of post-storm rodent movement becomes a lasting problem. Displaced animals sometimes move on once drier conditions return elsewhere, but a property with an existing entry point is more likely to see temporary displacement turn into an actual indoor population, which is the scenario an inspection and sealing visit is meant to prevent.

How this differs from the ordinary seasonal shift.

Cooler-weather displacement, discussed elsewhere on this site, tends to unfold gradually over several weeks as temperatures drop. Storm-driven displacement is faster and more concentrated, often arriving within days of a single flooding event rather than building slowly, which is why it is worth treating as its own distinct pattern rather than assuming it behaves the same way a seasonal shift does. A homeowner who already knows the signs of ordinary seasonal movement should still watch specifically for the sharper, faster spike a major storm can produce.

Before you call: safe steps for rodent signs

This is general public-health guidance from the CDC, not a description of any one job. It applies whether you call us or anyone else.

  • Never dry sweep or vacuum droppings, urine stains or nesting material.
  • Ventilate a shut-up space first. Half an hour of airing out is the CDC's starting point.
  • Gloves on first, handwashing last. Do both every time.
  • Soak the droppings and the surface in disinfectant first. Wiping comes only after the soak time.
  • Seal used paper towels and any dead rodent in a bag before disposal. Keep bare skin out of it.
  • Any fever, aches or breathing trouble after handling rodent waste should be checked by a doctor. We do not make health claims about individual exposure.

Common questions

Does every storm cause a rodent problem?

No, but heavy flooding does displace rodents from their usual outdoor areas, and a property with an open entry point is more likely to see that displacement turn into an indoor problem.

Should I seal entry points before or after storm season?

Before, if possible. Sealing ahead of a known risk period is simpler than dealing with an active population that moved in during a storm.

How soon after a storm should I check for new activity?

Within the days immediately following, especially if your property is near water or a low-lying drainage area that could have flooded nearby burrows.

Is it safe to check my yard right after a storm?

Only once conditions are actually safe, clear of standing water and storm debris. Any displacement activity will still be there to assess once it is safe to walk the property.
